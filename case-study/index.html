How it plays

Familiar loop, hero-sized twist

The roll-capture-build rhythm of MONOPOLY GO!, with the powers and rivalries of a superhero game. Matches run 8 rounds against three rivals.

  1. Roll and moveTwo dice move your hero around the city.
  2. Capture and buildOutpost, Tower, HQ: worth 1, 3 and 6 Empire Points.
  3. Use an abilityOne per turn, before the roll.
  4. Clash for controlOn a rival’s district: pay the toll or fight.
  5. Win in 8 roundsMost Empire Points rules Nova City.
The signature moment

The Empire Clash

A 10–15 second timing duel when two heroes fight over a district. Stop the needle in the gold zone three times.

  • Win: capture the district, or knock it down a level, and earn +2 EP.
  • Lose: pay double toll.
  • When a rival attacks you, the same meter becomes your block.

How I made it

From a one-page idea to a pitch-ready game

I ran this the way I’d lead a character team: I set the direction, made the art, reviewed every build and decided what changed. Claude was my engineer, writing the game, running the balance simulations and turning my art into game assets.

What I did

Concept and creative direction, the seven-hero roster, all character sheets, key art, environment and live-ops props, ability design, and review of every build.

What Claude did

Game code and rules, the Clash minigame, AI opponents, 700-match balance simulations, cropping my sheets into portraits, and the pitch deck layout.

  1. Pick a direction

    It started as one page: a superhero take on a dice-and-property game, with seven heroes fighting for a city. The first real decision was what kind of game it should be.

    A MONOPOLY GO! clone with powers would be easy to explain but forgettable. I chose a superhero strategy game with dice and territory control, where your hero changes how you play the board.

    Roll the dice. Unleash your powers. Conquer the city.
    Monopoly GO! + powersFamiliar, but a reskin.
    Strategy + territoryHeroes change the strategy.

  2. Key art, then a playable build

    The first key art set the tone: a bright floating city, a board ring and seven heroes. From it I had a playable prototype the same afternoon: a 20-tile board, dice, district upgrades, AI rivals and the Clash timing duel.

    The first build cropped its hero portraits straight from this key art, so the game had faces before the characters were final.

  3. A full sheet for every hero

    Each hero got a production sheet: a five-view turnaround, expressions, chibi emotes, gear callouts, three abilities and three skins. As the sheets came in, the game swapped in their art.

    The sheets also exposed overlaps across the roster: two heroes shared a hex-dome icon, two had a clone ability and two used crosshairs. Those became notes for the next icon pass.

  4. Turn 21 ability icons into rules

    Every ability on the sheets became a real mechanic. Some target a tile, like Hack, Mark or Deploy Node. Some protect, like Barrier or Lockdown. Some bend movement, like Boost, Phase Dash or Reposition.

    Each one shows what it does before you commit, and targeted ones light up the tiles you can pick. Nodes, Marks and sealed districts each read differently on the board.

  5. Balance with 700 simulated matches

    To check the roster, Claude ran 700 matches with AI players only. Two heroes fell behind: Catalyst averaged 5.6 Empire Points and The Sentinel 6.3, while the leaders scored nearly 10.

    The fix kept each hero’s identity. The Sentinel now earns +1 EP per turn while holding 3+ districts, which rewards turtling. Catalyst’s Nodes and Reaction now pay EP as well as credits. After the change, every hero averages between 8 and 10.

  6. Repaint the cover to match the roster

    Once all seven sheets existed, the first key art no longer matched. Catalyst and Arcstar had been redesigned, Neon’s look had changed, and Astra Vex wasn’t on it at all.

    I repainted the cover from the final sheets so every hero on the box is the hero you play.

    Key art v1Final cover

  7. Package it as a pitch

    Last, everything went into a 19-slide pitch deck: the hook, the match loop, the Clash, Nova City, one slide per hero sheet, the target screen, live-ops, art direction, the production pipeline and the prototype.

    What I learned: a playable build answers questions a deck can’t. Balancing in simulation took minutes and changed two heroes before anyone had to play a bad match.
